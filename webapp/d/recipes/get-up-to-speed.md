<webui-quote theme="title">
A new topic can be overwhelming: too many articles, too much jargon, and no idea what actually matters. Tell your assistant what you need to know and why, and it will give you the basics, the parts that matter for you, and what to check.
</webui-quote>

## What you'll need

<webui-page-segment elevation="10">

- The topic, in a few words.
- Why you need to know: a decision, a meeting, a project, or just curiosity. This shapes everything.
- An honest idea of what you already know, even if it's "almost nothing".

</webui-page-segment>

## How to use it

<webui-page-segment elevation="10">

1. **Fill in the blanks.** "Why you need to know" matters most. "I'm deciding whether to replace my furnace" gets a far more useful answer than "I'm interested in heat pumps".
1. **Copy the prompt** and paste it into a new chat. If your assistant has a web search option, turn it on, so the answer can include recent information.
1. **Ask follow-up questions.** "What does that term mean?", "Can you give an example?", "What questions should I ask an installer?"
1. **Check the important points** with the sources it suggests, or with someone who knows the field.

</webui-page-segment>

## Tips from your squire

<webui-page-segment elevation="10">

- **Ask for the other side.** "What would a skeptic say?" or "What are the most common mistakes people make?" often teaches you the most.
- **Set the level.** If the answer is too technical, say "explain it like I'm new to this". If it's too basic, say what you already know.
- **Open the sources.** Assistants can cite sources that don't exist or don't say what they claim. Click through before you rely on one.
- **Watch the dates.** Prices, rules, and products change. Ask "Is any of this likely to be out of date?" and check anything time-sensitive.
- **Don't stop at the AI.** For decisions about health, money, or the law, use what you learn to ask a professional better questions.

</webui-page-segment>

## An example

<webui-page-segment elevation="10">

A homeowner with an old furnace wants to know whether a heat pump makes sense. They fill in "heat pumps for an older house", "I'm deciding whether to replace my furnace", and "almost nothing". The answer explains how heat pumps work, then focuses on what matters for an older house: insulation, cold winters, and the cost of running one compared with gas. It suggests checking local rebates and getting quotes from installers. They follow up with "What should I ask an installer?" and take the list to three quotes.
